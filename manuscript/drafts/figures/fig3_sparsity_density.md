# Figure 3: The Sparsity-Density Paradox

**Claim**: The set of collapsible states is sparse relative to the total state space but becomes dense on the Bloch sphere as the detector size grows.

**One-sentence takeaway**: As $N$ increases, the number of discrete collapsible states grows, filling the Bloch sphere and transforming a sparse point process into a continuous Born-like measure.

**Question answered**: How can a finite set of points represent a continuous probability distribution?

**Panel flow**:
1. **The Point Process**: Three Bloch sphere renderings showing the distribution of roots for $N=5, 10, 15$, illustrating the increase in density.
2. **Coverage Scaling**: A plot of the "Coverage Gate" (fraction of bins with at least one root) vs. $N$.
3. **Measure Convergence**: A comparison of the empirical root-count ratio $R(\theta)$ against the Born curve as $N$ grows, showing the reduction in "graininess."

**Dominant panel**: The series of Bloch spheres showing the transition from sparse points to a dense cloud.

**Instant comparison**: The contrast between the "gappy" distribution at small $N$ and the "smooth" coverage at large $N$.

**Quantitative anchor**: The transition point where coverage reaches $1.0$ (e.g., $N=17$).

**Alternative explanation addressed**: This proves that "Born-like" is an asymptotic property of the root process, not a property of a single finite-size instance.

**Why main text**: This addresses the "sparse yet dense" essence of the theory, bridging the gap between discrete algebraic roots and continuous probability.
