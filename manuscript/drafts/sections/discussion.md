# Discussion

The exact result is a projective classification, not a collapse mechanism. For any finite qubit--detector unitary, pole-compatible product inputs are roots of homogeneous detector-space pencils. Regular pencils have $d$ projective roots counted with algebraic multiplicity, and unitarity fixes the two outcome-labelled multisets as exact Bloch antipodes. Haar propagators sharpen the comparison: their roots obey the complex spherical-ensemble law and have uniform one-point intensity, so an ensemble-selected axis is absent without invoking a large-$d$ approximation.

The matched spin ring supplies a structured counterpoint to that null. Its $N=16,t=10^4$ full-sphere map is strongly dipolar at the audited resolution, and the four-time median polar score increases monotonically across $N=11,\ldots,16$. These observations show that this finite many-body model organizes projective root counts in a Born-like direction. They do not establish why the matched-field condition works, whether the trend persists under controlled enlargement of size and time windows, or whether higher odd multipoles vanish asymptotically. The data therefore motivate mechanism and scaling studies rather than a universal claim.

Three operational gaps remain decisive. First, algebraic multiplicity supplies no normalized preparation or selection measure over qubit roots, detector null rays, degeneracies, and readout times. Second, different roots generally require different detector microstates, whereas a measurement apparatus should accept a nontrivial family of qubit inputs from a common ready macrostate. Third, factorization at one time does not establish stable, distinguishable, or redundantly encoded detector records. Production-scale homogeneous-QZ residuals and conditioning diagnostics also remain to be completed before submission-level numerical certification.

These limitations define the next tests. A physical measure must be specified independently of the target Born profile; detector factors must be reconstructed and tested for record distinguishability and persistence; the matched sequence must be repeated with homogeneous projective solvers and preregistered size, time, and resolution choices; and the matched-field condition needs an independent predictor tested on held-out configurations. Until those tests succeed, the defensible conclusion is narrower but concrete: projective outcome geometry turns one part of the unitary-measurement question into a mathematically defined many-body classification problem.
